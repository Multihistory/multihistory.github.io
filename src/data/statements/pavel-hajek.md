My involvement began at the Wolfram Winter School 2025, where I worked on the insertion Lie bracket suggested by Carlos and Xerxes. I generalized it to hypergraphs, using a hyperedge as the root, and uncovered its relation to the commutator of linearized “elementary blow-up” rewriting rules. I defined graded versions and versions for different edge symmetries, and started exploring higher-arity constructions from hypergraph gluing, as well as the representability of Lie algebras on subsets of rules and subspaces of the hypergraph vector space. I implemented some of these ideas in code and contributed to the Hypergraph paclet.

In October 2025, I joined the Wolfram Institute, funded by the JTF grant. After some initial contributions to the Wolfram Function Repository, the DiscreteGeometry paclet, and the PvsNP blog project, I started to work under the direction of Stephen Wolfram on the Institute's Infrageometry project, which was broadly understood as building a version of discrete geometry that could be useful in the Wolfram Physics project.

After initial meetings with Stephen and Nik Murzin, where I absorbed the main philosophy and vision of the computational universe and learned about causal graphs, branchial graphs, spatial reconstruction, and the concept of computational minimal models, I proposed a concrete pipeline for obtaining an emergent spacetime from a single-history branch of the multiway system of hypergraph rewriting. The pipeline starts with a given observer chain and foliation, together with nearby observer chains whose intersections with the foliation represent germs based at the original observer. It produces spatial slices fibered over ℤ³, with gauge degrees of freedom and coarse-graining maps. I then committed to developing a mathematically sound narrative and tools for this proposal.

I implemented the idea of observer-scale dependence in minimal models of spatial geometry and arrived at the notion of renormalizable geometry. Discreteness is characterized by non-uniqueness. I introduced vertex and edge densities, counting how often individual vertices and edges occur across different representatives of one geometric concept. I explored how these densities could lead to discrete notions of synthetic geometry, with geometric constructions allowed to branch rather than produce a unique result, and formulated various research questions around this idea. In another direction, I worked on describing the discrete substrate itself in geometric terms and on notions of convergence connecting these discrete descriptions with continuous geometry, offering an alternative view of Riemannian geometry as a graph limit.

I also implemented a minimal model of fibered graphs, sections, and connections, compatible with elementary Wolfram Language structures and, together with my student collaborator Ioana Milea, began exploring its connection to lattice gauge theory, viewed as a “mesoscale theory.” Her previous work on this topic provided important inspiration for this direction.

For infracalculus, I viewed directed graphs, which are naturally fibered over ℤ by shortest-path distance from their sources, as discrete models of the real line. I developed integration and differentiation of vertex labels as different versions of aggregation and its left inverse. I studied the renormalizability of these notions under various blow-up constructions and contractions in the radial direction. I also studied various methods of coordinatization, meaning the assignment of tuples of labels to vertices in a natural way given certain synthetic objects, such as orthogonal axes. These methods included shortest-path projection, parallel transport, and radar coordinates. I studied their properties and tried to develop a sensible notion of discrete multivariate calculus.

In parallel with this work, I developed several research repositories that explore ideas within the broader vision of a computable universe advocated by Stephen Wolfram and are mostly part of the JTF public deliverables.

My repositories combine my original code with contributions from Nik Murzin, Dugan Hammock, and student collaborators, as well as code later developed with the assistance of AI tools. I wanted to use the paclet documentation and the language design itself to communicate the theoretical structure.

I am very grateful to Stephen Wolfram, Nik Murzin, Dugan Hammock, Carlos, Xerxes Arsiwalla, Ioana Milea, and other members of the Institute for sharing their perspectives, ideas, discussions, and suggestions throughout this work. I was also positively influenced and motivated by my mentees and acquaintances at the Wolfram Summer School and Winter School, which provided a great environment for exchanging ideas and sharing our enthusiasm for this novel view of science.

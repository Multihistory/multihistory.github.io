My involvement began at the Wolfram Winter School 2025, where I worked on the insertion Lie bracket suggested by Carlos and Xerxes. I generalized the construction to hypergraphs, using a hyperedge as the root, with a unary edge being the analogue of a vertex root, and uncovered its relation to the commutator of linearized “elementary blow-up” rewriting rules. I then developed graded versions and variants for different edge symmetries, and began exploring higher-arity constructions arising from hypergraph gluing, as well as representations of Lie algebras on subsets of rewriting rules and on subspaces of the vector space generated by isomorphism classes of rooted hypergraphs. I implemented several of these constructions in code and contributed to the Hypergraph paclet.

In October 2025, I joined the Wolfram Institute, funded by Carlos's JTF grant. After some initial contributions to the Wolfram Function Repository, the DiscreteGeometry paclet, and the PvsNP blog project, I began working under the direction of Stephen Wolfram on the Institute’s Infrageometry project. The broader goal of the project was to develop a form of discrete geometry that could be useful for the Wolfram Physics Project.

After initial meetings with Stephen and Nik Murzin, where I absorbed the main philosophy and vision of the computational universe and learned about causal graphs, branchial graphs, spatial reconstruction, and computational minimal models, I proposed a concrete minimal-model pipeline for obtaining an emergent spacetime from a single-history branch of the multiway system of hypergraph rewriting. The construction starts with a given observer chain and corresponding foliation, together with nearby observer chains whose intersections with the foliation represent germs based at the original observer. These give rise to rectilinear coordinates, so that the spatially reconstructed slices become fibered over ℤ³, leading to spatial and gauge degrees of freedom. Coarse-graining maps between the slices represent persistence in time and allow us to talk about a limiting space. I then committed to developing a mathematically sound narrative and the necessary tools for realizing this proposal computationally.

First, I designed and implemented a minimal model of fibered graphs, sections, and connections, compatible with elementary Wolfram Language structures. Together with my student collaborator Ioana Milea, we began exploring its connection to lattice gauge theory, viewed as a “mesoscale theory” obtainable by aggregation of my minimal model. Her previous work on discrete gauge theory provided important inspiration for this direction.

Next, I wanted to understand how geometry might emerge when a computationally bounded observer measures a large graph at a coarse scale. This led me to the notion of renormalizable geometry, based on scale-dependent discrete minimal models and scaled Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of graphs, viewed as metric-measure spaces, together with weak convergence of their observables. I explored this concretely by developing a computational language for synthetic Euclidean geometry on graphs. I introduced vertex and edge densities that measure how many representatives of a geometric concept, such as the shortest paths representing a segment, contain a given vertex or edge, and designed fast algorithms for computing them in constructions such as line, arc, and geodesic extension. I then investigated how these observables behave across scales and might converge when the underlying graphs approach a Euclidean-like limit.

After reading Riemann’s original habilitation lecture, I began to see space as a webbing of paths and realized that an approach to Riemannian geometry through graph limits may be very natural. In this approach, infinitesimality is not imposed from the beginning, but emerges as a feature of the effective description of a large graph at large scales, rather than starting with an infinitesimal manifold structure and an infinitesimal metric tensor. This also connected naturally to the volume-measure experiments from the original Wolfram Physics Project introduction, which we wanted to investigate more closely with Stephen, in particular the reconstruction of geometric invariants from such measurements. I began designing computational experiments around the question of what measurements an observer operating at a scale r can perform, what geometric properties can be reconstructed from those measurements, and under what conditions familiar consequences of infinitesimality, such as linearity, and corresponding objects, such as tensors, might emerge. For example, my Summer School mentee Merna Youseff studied how the distribution of mutual graph distances encodes the shape of a discretized ellipsoid.

I considered directed graphs, naturally fibered over ℤ by shortest-path distance from their sources, as discrete models of the real line. I developed notions of integration and differentiation of functions represented by vertex labels as different forms of aggregation and their corresponding left inverses. I studied the renormalizability of these notions under various blow-up constructions and contractions in the radial direction. I also investigated several methods of coordinatization, meaning the natural assignment of tuples of labels to vertices given certain synthetic geometric structures, such as orthogonal axes. These methods included shortest-path projection, parallel transport, and radar coordinates. I studied their properties and tried to develop from them a sensible notion of computational discrete multivariate calculus.

I proposed scale-dependent notions of topology and ways of measuring them, such as through ball-intersection complexes, interpolating between Čech and Vietoris–Rips complexes in one parameter and varying the scale in another. I also developed discrete analogues of tangent and cotangent bundles, a discrete canonical one-form at scale r, a Levi-Civita connection at scale r, and many other constructions. I implemented computational versions of several of these constructions in the Infrageometry repository, with significant effort devoted to developing a convenient and natural language, with natural parameters, for doing geometry on graphs. The largely unfinished repositories became part of the public JTF deliverables before my fellowship at the Wolfram Institute ended.

In parallel with this work, I started developing several other research repositories exploring ideas within the broader vision of a computable universe advocated by Stephen Wolfram, which also became part of the public JTF deliverables. These included a repository on discrete causality; a registry of hypergraph rewriting rules together with computational experiments studying their emergent geometric properties and mapping their landscape based on these observables; a rewrite of Euclid’s Elements in terms of the synthetic geometry on graphs; and other projects not directly related to geometry, but motivated by my involvement in Institute activities, including UniversalityDB and IteratedFiniteAutomata.

My repositories combine my original code with contributions from other members of the Institute, in particular Nik Murzin, Dugan Hammock, and student collaborators. Throughout the year, I increasingly used AI for exploratory coding and developed a Claude plugin to capture and organize my computational research workflow. For scientific communication, I experimented with using paclet documentation and language design to communicate the theory instead of relying only on conventional papers. I saw this as a more direct way to combine mathematical exposition with executable code while keeping the work open to experimentation and contributions.

I was also positively influenced and motivated by my mentees and acquaintances at the Wolfram Summer School and Winter School, which provided a great environment for exchanging ideas and sharing our enthusiasm for this novel view of science.
